# Worked Examples: From Decision to Draft

These examples show how the system's documents connect. They demonstrate process rather than finished chapters.

## Nonfiction example: Strength Under Control

[BIBLICAL PROJECT]
The project is a 60,000-word nonfiction book defining biblical manhood for sons and young men ages 13-21. The primary reader is sixteen or seventeen. The chapter **Strength Under Control** sits in the foundations section.

### Book promise excerpt

Help young men understand biblical manhood as identity expressed through responsibility, courage, restraint, service, integrity, and faithful action, using Scripture in context and realistic modern situations.

### Project-bible rules used

- Challenge without shame.
- Direct quotations must be checked against the named Bible translation.
- Observation, interpretation, theological choice, and application remain distinct.
- The main prose speaks to the midpoint reader; examples vary by age.
- Sensitive guidance directs readers toward trustworthy adults and qualified help.
- Strength is never equated with domination, silence, or immunity to fear.

### Architecture entry

Part II establishes foundations. Earlier chapters define identity and responsibility. This chapter distinguishes controlled strength from domination and passivity. Later chapters apply the distinction to anger, relationships, authority, and leadership.

### Chapter brief

**Primary job:** Replace the idea that strength means domination with strength governed by love, wisdom, and responsibility.

**Beginning state:** The reader may associate manhood with physical power, emotional hardness, winning, or control.

**Ending state:** The reader can identify restraint as an active use of strength and choose one restrained response in a realistic conflict.

**Internal moves:**

1. Online humiliation and the impulse to retaliate
2. Why retaliation feels like strength
3. Source and Scripture work completed by the author
4. Restraint versus passivity
5. A complication involving danger or abuse
6. A practical response process
7. Reflection and transition to courage

**Exclusions:** No diagnosis, legal advice, invented testimony, universal promise, or unverified quotation.

**Review required:** Theology, Scripture context, youth suitability, and safety.

### Context-reset packet excerpt

> We are drafting only the section "Restraint is not passivity." The reader already understands responsibility as ownership of the next faithful action. Explain that restraint can include speaking, reporting harm, setting a boundary, or seeking protection. Do not imply that enduring abuse is virtuous. Use no direct Bible quotation in this section. Write 450-550 words and end with the question: how can a young man know when to wait and when to act?

### Draft review

Suppose the generated draft says: "A strong man never lets another person's words control his emotions."

The sentence should not be approved unchanged.

- **Function:** It attempts to define restraint.
- **Reasoning problem:** "Never" creates an impossible standard and treats emotional response as voluntary control.
- **Audience problem:** A struggling reader may hear shame rather than agency.
- **Revision direction:** Separate feeling from action. The reader may feel anger and still choose a response.

A better author-controlled direction is: "Restraint does not mean you feel nothing. It means anger does not get the final vote on what you do next."

### Ledger update

- Definition added: restraint is active governance of response, not absence of emotion.
- Safety note added: restraint does not require remaining silent in danger or abuse.
- Later dependency: Chapter 16 applies this definition to conflict and repair.
- Voice note: avoid "real men never" formulas.

### Chapter handoff excerpt

The chapter establishes that strength becomes trustworthy when it is governed. It distinguishes restraint from passivity and prepares the reader for courage as faithful action under fear. The anger chapter must preserve the feeling/action distinction. The leadership chapter must not redefine strength as influence or status.

## Fiction example: Mara and the Flood Map

[FICTION]
Mara is a teenage mapmaker in a city that floods higher each year. She believes her missing brother abandoned the family. When a coded map appears after a workshop flood, she must work with Tomas, the rival apprentice she blames for the disappearance.

### Fiction promise

A mystery adventure about a young mapmaker who must choose truth over vindication while uncovering why her brother vanished and who benefits from the city's rising water.

### Project-bible rules used

- Viewpoint stays close third person with Mara except for two approved interludes.
- Flood levels and travel times follow the city map.
- Mara notices spatial details before emotional ones.
- Tomas knows the brother investigated the gates but does not know why he disappeared.
- The coded map cannot reveal the final answer before Chapter 17.

### Chapter brief

**Chapter 6 job:** Force Mara to accept Tomas as a temporary partner.

**Beginning state:** Mara has the map but cannot read the tide notation. She believes Tomas helped her brother leave.

**Scene goal:** Reach the old gauge house before the evening surge.

**Obstacle:** The safe bridge is closed, and Tomas knows an illegal route.

**Choice:** Mara can follow him or lose the day's only access window.

**Ending state:** She follows him but secretly marks the route in case he betrays her.

**Continuity:** Mara's left hand is bandaged; Tomas has not seen the full map; sunset surge begins at 18:40.

### Context-reset packet excerpt

> Draft only the crossing scene from Mara's viewpoint. Preserve her bandaged left hand and tendency to notice space before emotion. Tomas must solve the route problem without becoming suddenly charming. Mara chooses to follow because the time window closes, not because she trusts him. Do not reveal what Tomas knows about her brother. End when Mara notices he has deliberately taken the more dangerous position.

### Draft review

If the draft lets Mara leap between roofs using both hands, it fails physical continuity. If Tomas confesses his entire connection to the brother, it violates the reveal plan. If Mara follows because she senses he is "good deep down," the scene replaces a pressured choice with unsupported intuition.

The approved version must preserve motive, knowledge, body, geography, and ending state.

### Tracker update

- Mara learns Tomas knows the illegal canal routes.
- Tomas sees only the upper-left corner of the coded map.
- Mara marks Route C in charcoal.
- The bandage becomes wet and must matter in Chapter 7.
- Setup added: Tomas takes the exposed position without explanation.
- Payoff scheduled: Chapter 11 reveals why he fears deep water.

## What both examples prove

The genres differ, but the operating logic is the same:

1. The project bible establishes stable rules.
2. Architecture gives the chapter a job.
3. The brief defines movement and boundaries.
4. The context packet supplies only relevant memory.
5. Drafting occurs in a controlled unit.
6. Review tests function before polish.
7. Trackers preserve what changed.
8. The handoff prepares the next chapter.

The AI assistant contributes options and prose. The author controls meaning, truth, continuity, and approval.
