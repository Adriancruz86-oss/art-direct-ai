# Art Direct AI — Pass 3 Copy Build
## Part IV: Art Direct the Page, Not Just the Picture
Date: 2026-09-05
Status: working final copy

Editorial purpose: move the reader from generating attractive standalone pictures to directing illustrations that function as a sequence of book pages.

---

# 15 — Composition Before Decoration

A beautiful image can still be a bad book page.

The generator does not automatically know where your words need to go, what the reader should notice first, or what the next page is doing.

Those are page decisions.

Before generating, answer three questions:

1. **What is the story beat?**
2. **Where should the reader look first?**
3. **Where do the words need to live?**

Only then should you worry about making the scene attractive.

## Give the page one visual priority

If the important beat is the child discovering a broken toy, the composition should make that discovery easy to read.

The room does not need equal attention.

The window does not need equal attention.

The rug does not need equal attention.

A useful direction might be:

> Child seated low on the right, looking toward the broken toy near center. Keep the upper-left third mostly open for text. Background furniture suggested only with a few broad shapes.

That is more useful than:

> Beautiful cozy bedroom, whimsical children's-book style.

The second request asks for atmosphere.

The first directs a page.

## Protect the focal point

Watch for accidental competitors:

- a bright window behind the character;
- a highly detailed plant;
- a glowing lamp;
- a patterned rug;
- a second face in the background;
- a saturated object that has nothing to do with the story.

If the reader notices the decoration before the story beat, the hierarchy is wrong.

### Composition test

Blur or shrink the page.

Can you still tell where the eye is supposed to go?

If not, adding more detail will not fix it.

# 16 — Vary Your Camera

Generators love a comfortable distance.

Give them enough freedom and many pages settle into a similar medium-wide view: character visible, environment visible, everything nicely centered and readable.

One page may look good.

Twenty pages begin to feel generated.

## Change distance on purpose

Use a small working vocabulary:

### WIDE
Use when place, scale or isolation matters.

The character may be small. The environment carries part of the story.

### MEDIUM
Use for interaction, conversation and clear physical action.

Useful — but easy to overuse.

### CLOSE
Use when emotion or a small reaction deserves emphasis.

Save it for moments that earn it.

### DETAIL
Hands holding an object. A muddy shoe. A torn drawing. A key in a lock.

The character does not always need to be fully visible.

## Sequence the distances

Do not choose each shot independently.

Look at the page before and the page after.

If you have:

**medium → medium → medium**

ask whether the sequence would be stronger as:

**wide → medium → close**

or:

**medium → detail → wide**

Variation creates rhythm without adding decoration.

## Do not manufacture action

A different camera angle is often enough to make a page feel fresh.

You do not need to make the character run, leap or twist simply because the previous page was static.

Complex movement introduces anatomy risk.

Use movement when the manuscript requires movement.

Use composition when you merely need visual variety.

# 17 — Design for the Words

Do not generate the illustration first and then search for somewhere to squeeze the manuscript.

Text is part of the page from the beginning.

## "Leave room for text" is too vague

Tell the composition where that room belongs.

For example:

> Keep the upper-left third as mostly untouched warm paper. Character low and right. No important objects, faces or strong contrast behind the text area.

That gives the model a spatial job.

## Text space still needs composition

A blank rectangle cut out of a busy illustration can look accidental.

The open area should feel connected to the image.

Use:

- fading washes;
- broad quiet shapes;
- natural sky or wall areas;
- directional gaze toward or away from the text;
- asymmetrical placement that balances the words and picture.

## Do not bake final words into generated art

Generated typography is not final typography.

Headings, manuscript copy, captions, page numbers and labels should be typeset normally.

This gives you:

- correct spelling;
- consistent fonts;
- editable line breaks;
- proper margins;
- real text-safe control;
- accessibility and production flexibility.

Generate **space for words**, not the finished words.

## The text-space test

Place a plain gray block where the manuscript will go.

Does the illustration still feel intentionally composed?

If the page only works before the words arrive, it was never finished.

# 18 — Two Pages Are One Composition

A spread is not two unrelated pictures sitting beside each other.

When a book is open, the reader sees both pages at once.

That means the center matters.

## The gutter is a danger zone

Do not place critical information where binding may swallow or distort it.

Avoid putting these directly across the center unless you have intentionally designed for it:

- eyes;
- faces;
- small hands;
- important props;
- tiny text;
- precise character features;
- anything the reader must read clearly.

A large background shape can cross the gutter.

A face split down the nose usually should not.

## Compose across the spread

Ask:

- Does the eye move naturally from one page to the other?
- Is one side accidentally much heavier?
- Does the text compete with the focal image?
- Does the center interrupt anything important?
- Does the composition still work after realistic trim and binding?

## Flat design first

Do not rely on fake photographed-book mockups, center shadows or generated page curls to tell you whether a spread works.

Build the actual pages flat.

Use a real gutter guide during layout.

Then preview the spread as an open book.

### Production note

Exact bleed, safe-zone and binding requirements depend on the printer and format. Later in the guide, the production reference distinguishes common practice from platform-specific requirements.

# 19 — Make the Page Turn Do Something

A page turn is a storytelling tool.

Do not reveal everything one page too early.

## Three useful page-turn jobs

### REVEAL
Before the turn: anticipation or partial information.

After the turn: the thing itself.

A child hears something enormous outside.

Do not show the enormous thing yet.

Turn the page.

Now show it.

### CONTRAST
Before: crowded, noisy, dark, tense.

After: open, quiet, bright, relieved.

The physical turn strengthens the change.

### PUNCHLINE
Before: setup.

After: visual answer, surprise or joke.

Especially useful in younger books where timing is part of the fun.

## Keep the setup disciplined

AI often wants to make the setup page impressive too.

Resist that.

If the next spread contains the reveal, the setup may need to be smaller, quieter or more incomplete.

Do not spend the climax's visual budget before the reader turns the page.

### Page-turn question

Before approving a spread, ask:

> **What does the reader know now, and what am I saving for after the turn?**

If the answer is "everything," you may have thrown away the reveal.

# 20 — Page Turn: Before & After

Use one strong worked example. Do not add a second redundant cabin/reveal example.

## BEFORE THE TURN

The manuscript establishes anticipation.

Art direction:
- smaller composition;
- restrained detail;
- character looking toward the source of the mystery;
- open space;
- do not show the answer;
- keep visual intensity below the next spread.

The page should make the reader want to turn it.

## AFTER THE TURN

Now spend the visual budget.

Art direction:
- wider or more dominant composition;
- reveal fully readable;
- scale contrast clear;
- text placed where it does not weaken the reveal;
- detail concentrated on the story event rather than decorative surroundings.

## What changed?

Not merely the prompt.

The **job of the page** changed.

The first page withholds.

The second delivers.

That is art direction across a sequence.
