# PAGES 16–17 — DECIDE WHAT CANNOT CHANGE

# LOCK IDENTITY BEFORE POSES.

A generator can keep the *idea* of a character while quietly redesigning the character.

Start with what the reader recognizes from across the room:

- apparent age;
- head-to-body proportion;
- overall silhouette;
- hair silhouette and length;
- face shape;
- relative height;
- core clothing silhouette;
- companion size and shape.

Tiny matching details cannot rescue a silhouette that has changed.

## THREE KINDS OF FEATURES

### IMMUTABLE
These define identity:
- apparent age;
- skin tone;
- body proportions;
- face structure;
- hair color, length, and primary silhouette;
- identity-defining features;
- core outfit when the story uses one;
- companion size, markings, and major features;
- relative height between recurring characters.

### SCENE-DEPENDENT
These change because the story requires them:
- pose;
- expression;
- gaze;
- temporary outerwear;
- story props;
- lighting response;
- temporary dirt, rain, or sleepiness.

### FLEXIBLE
These can vary slightly:
- exact placement of a loose hair mark;
- minor clothing folds;
- hand position within a similar pose;
- small pencil irregularities;
- tiny asymmetries.

> **The goal is not to freeze the character. It is to know what is allowed to move.**

## HAIR IS A SHAPE BEFORE IT IS HAIR

"Brown messy hair" leaves too much room.

Better:

> "Medium-brown hair forming one loose rounded mass, slightly wider above the ears, short at the neck, simple uneven fringe. Only a few internal pencil marks."

## PAGE-ONE TEST

Before finished pages begin, answer:

1. What does this character look like from across the room?
2. What five features cannot change?
3. What is the simplest version of the core outfit?
4. How much facial detail belongs in this book?
5. What does the character look like from the side?
6. What would count as unacceptable drift?

### ART / LAYOUT
Use canonical boy/dog large enough to inspect. One page establishes identity; second page handles immutable/scene-dependent/flexible with breathing room.

### ASSET
Approved canonical boy/dog material. No new generation.

# PAGES 18–19 — BUILD A REFERENCE SHEET YOU WILL ACTUALLY USE

# A REFERENCE SHEET IS A DECISION RECORD.

Its job is to answer the questions later generations are most likely to improvise.

## USEFUL VIEWS

For most children's-book characters, start with:

**Front · Three-quarter · Side**

Add a back view only when hair, clothing, or markings make it useful.

Add one seated view if the manuscript uses sitting frequently.

You do not need twenty poses.

## SMALL EXPRESSION RANGE

Show enough to establish the book's emotional ceiling:

**Neutral · Happy · Worried/Sad**

Add stronger reactions only when the story genuinely needs them.

A quiet hand-drawn character should not suddenly become a giant open-mouth cartoon reaction because the prompt says "excited."

Consistency includes **how emotion is rendered**.

## RECORD WHAT MATTERS

**Hair** — silhouette, length, fringe/part, major mass.  
**Face** — shape, spacing, amount of rendering.  
**Body** — proportions and relative height.  
**Clothing** — core silhouette and simple colors.  
**Shoes** — type and basic shape.  
**Companion** — size, muzzle, ears, tail, markings, collar.  
**Palette** — a few stable color anchors.

## DON'T OVER-REFERENCE

You usually do not need ten eye close-ups, every hand pose, dozens of expressions, exact fabric texture, or hundreds of hair marks.

Reference the things that preserve identity.

Leave ordinary drawing variation room to breathe.

# PAGES 21–22 — CONSISTENCY IS MORE THAN THE FACE

# CHECK THE WHOLE IDENTITY SYSTEM.

Creators often compare faces first.

But a character can fail even when the face looks close.

## 1 — APPARENT AGE
Watch head size, jaw shape, limb length, shoulder width, and facial maturity.

## 2 — SILHOUETTE + PROPORTIONS
Compare head/body ratio, torso length, leg length, shoulder width, overall height, and companion scale.

## 3 — HAIR
Compare the outer mass before individual marks: length, width, fringe, part, volume, and relationship to ears/jaw/neck.

## 4 — FACE SYSTEM
Check face shape, feature spacing, nose/mouth treatment, amount of detail, and emotional rendering ceiling.

## 5 — CLOTHING
Check garment type, silhouette, sleeve/hem length, core colors, and uninvited patterns/layers.

## 6 — SHOES + SMALL ANCHORS
Shoes, glasses, clips, collars, and similar anchors are easy for the model to redesign.

## 7 — COMPANION
Compare size, muzzle, ears, tail, coat length, markings, and collar/accessory.

> **If you need to explain why two versions are "technically the same character," the design has probably drifted too far.**

# PAGES 23–24 — CATCH DRIFT BEFORE IT SPREADS

# KEEP MODEL PAGES.

Choose two or three approved illustrations that best represent the character in the actual book:

- one clear face;
- one full-body view;
- one different angle or seated pose;
- companion together with the character when relevant.

Before approving a new generation, compare it against:

**1. Canonical reference sheet**  
**2. Model pages**  
**3. Neighboring pages**

The original prompt alone is not enough.

## COMPARE IN THE SAME ORDER

> **Silhouette → age/proportion → hair → face → clothing → shoes/details → companion → rendering style**

A repeatable order catches problems faster than staring at two images and asking whether they "feel the same."

## NAME THE DRIFT

Weak:

> "He looks different."

Useful:

> "His head is smaller relative to his body, his hair is longer at the neck, and the hoodie became a zip-up jacket."

Now you know what to repair.

## REPAIR THE VARIABLE

If the scene, composition, and medium work, do not discard them because the shoes changed.

Correct the shoes.

If the dog markings drifted, repair the dog.

If the hair silhouette changed, repair the hair.

Do not reopen successful decisions without a reason.

### CLEAN SILHOUETTES HELP
For consistency/reference work, uncomplicated poses are easier to compare and reduce avoidable anatomy failures.

### ART / LAYOUT
Worked spread: one model/canonical image plus obvious examples labeled **WHAT DRIFTED** and **WHAT TO REPAIR**. Real typography only.

### ASSET
Use existing drift examples and canonical art. No new whole-spread generation.
