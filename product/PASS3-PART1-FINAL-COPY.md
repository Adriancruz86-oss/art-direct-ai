# 1 — Stop Making AI-Looking Children's Books

**Opening hook**

AI is not the problem.

**Default AI is.**

A generator can give you a technically beautiful image in seconds: glowing light, perfect skin, detailed hair, decorative backgrounds, cinematic color, polished clothing.

And that is exactly the problem.

When thousands of people ask for a "beautiful children's-book illustration," the model is free to make most of the visual decisions. It reaches for familiar solutions. The images may be attractive, but they begin to feel interchangeable.

Your job is not to hide that AI was involved.

Your job is to stop accepting its first visual instincts as art direction.

**This guide teaches you how.**

> Do not ask the model to make the best image it can. Ask it to make the image your book needs.

**Layout note:** Let the opening poster/art carry most of the spread. Keep this copy short. Do not turn the hook into an essay.

# 2 — Why Everyone Is Getting the Same Look

Ask for:

> A beautiful whimsical children's-book illustration.

You have told the model almost nothing about the illustration you actually want.

You have not decided:

- how much detail belongs in the face;
- whether hair is a simple shape or hundreds of strands;
- whether clothing is plain or decorated;
- whether the background is suggested or fully rendered;
- whether empty paper should remain empty;
- how dramatic the lighting should be;
- what should receive the reader's attention;
- what the image must **not** become.

The generator has to answer those questions somehow.

So it falls back on visual patterns that reliably communicate *beautiful*, *whimsical*, *professional*, or *magical*.

That is where the familiar AI fingerprints come from:

**Too much finish. Too much detail. Too much lighting. Too much decoration. Too much happening everywhere.**

The model's default instinct is often **more**.

Children's-book illustration frequently needs the opposite.

### The useful question

Before you generate, ask:

**What decisions would annoy me if the model made them for me?**

Those decisions belong in your direction.

# 3 — You're the Art Director Now

Prompting tells the model **what is in the picture**.

Art direction tells it **what the picture is allowed to become**.

Suppose the manuscript calls for:

> A boy and his dog sit at the end of a dock at sunset.

That is the scene.

It does not tell us whether the finished page should contain:

- a spectacular orange sky;
- individual blades of grass;
- reflections across every inch of water;
- detailed hoodie folds;
- glossy eyes;
- rim light around the characters;
- decorative birds;
- flowers near the dock;
- a perfectly rendered horizon.

Those are visual decisions.

If the story does not need them, **do not surrender them to the model.**

An art director decides:

### What matters
The boy and dog sharing a quiet moment.

### What stays simple
Clothing, distant shoreline, water, sky.

### What remains empty
Areas of raw paper and space reserved for words.

### What cannot drift
Character age, proportions, hair mass, outfit, dog markings, medium.

### What is prohibited
No cinematic glow. No ornate sky. No decorative foliage. No glossy character rendering.

This is the shift the rest of the guide teaches:

**Generate less blindly. Decide more deliberately.**

# 4 — Diagnose Your Illustration

Do not regenerate an image just because it "looks AI."

Name the problem.

### 1. Too much detail
Hair, fabric, foliage, props, textures and tiny surfaces all receive attention.

**Fix:** lower the detail ceiling.

### 2. Over-rendered faces
Glossy eyes, smooth skin, perfect gradients, lashes, lip highlights and cosmetic-looking blush overpower a loose illustration.

**Fix:** reduce the face to the marks the expression actually needs.

### 3. Character drift
Age, face shape, proportions, hair, clothing or companion design changes between pages.

**Fix:** compare against a canonical reference and established model pages.

### 4. Decorative clutter
The model fills empty areas with flowers, stars, shelves, pillows, plants, particles or props because empty space looks unfinished to it.

**Fix:** protect negative space explicitly.

### 5. Style drift
The character may be correct, but pencil becomes digital linework, watercolor becomes airbrushed shading, or a loose page suddenly becomes polished concept art.

**Fix:** restore the rendering rules rather than rewriting the scene.

### The one-sentence diagnosis rule

Before you regenerate, complete this sentence:

> **The image fails because __________.**

If the answer is only *I don't like it*, you are not ready to regenerate.

The rest of this book teaches you how to fill in that blank.
