# PAGE 26 — COMPOSITION BEFORE DECORATION

# A BEAUTIFUL IMAGE CAN STILL BE A BAD BOOK PAGE.

Before generating, answer:

**1. What is the story beat?**  
**2. Where should the reader look first?**  
**3. Where do the words need to live?**

Then direct the page.

Instead of:

> "Beautiful cozy bedroom, whimsical children's-book style."

Try:

> "Child seated low on the right, looking toward the broken toy near center. Keep the upper-left third mostly open for text. Background furniture suggested with only a few broad shapes."

The second instruction does not merely request atmosphere.

It establishes hierarchy.

## WATCH FOR ACCIDENTAL COMPETITORS
A bright window. A glowing lamp. A patterned rug. A detailed plant. A saturated object unrelated to the story.

If decoration gets noticed before the story beat, the hierarchy is wrong.

> **Shrink the page. Can you still tell where the eye should go?**

Use retained composition art. Simplify secondary thumbnails if necessary.

# PAGE 27 — VARY YOUR CAMERA

# DON'T LET EVERY PAGE BECOME THE SAME MEDIUM-WIDE SHOT.

## WIDE
Use when place, scale, or isolation matters.

## MEDIUM
Useful for interaction and clear physical action. Easy to overuse.

## CLOSE
Use when emotion or reaction deserves emphasis.

## DETAIL
Hands holding an object. A muddy shoe. A torn drawing. A key in a lock.

The character does not always need to be fully visible.

## THINK IN SEQUENCES

Instead of:

**medium → medium → medium**

consider:

**wide → medium → close**

or:

**medium → detail → wide**

A different camera can create visual variety without inventing unnecessary action.

> **Vary the view before you make the character run.**

### ART / LAYOUT
Four examples maximum. Large enough to read instantly.

# PAGE 28 — DESIGN FOR THE WORDS

# GENERATE SPACE FOR WORDS.
## NOT THE WORDS THEMSELVES.

"Leave room for text" is too vague.

Direct where that room belongs:

> "Keep the upper-left third mostly untouched warm paper. Character low and right. No faces, important objects, or strong contrast behind the text area."

The open area should still belong to the composition.

Useful text space can be created with:

- fading washes;
- broad quiet shapes;
- natural sky or wall areas;
- intentional asymmetry;
- gaze and movement that support the page.

## TYPESET NORMALLY

Final headings, manuscript copy, captions, labels, measurements, and page numbers should not be generated inside the illustration.

Real typography gives you correct spelling, editable line breaks, consistent fonts, proper margins, and production control.

> **If the page only works before the words arrive, it was never finished.**

### ART / LAYOUT
One restrained illustration with a visible real text-safe overlay during design; overlay removed or softened in final teaching presentation.

### ASSET
Harvest retained composition/text-space art. No new dashboard generation.

# PAGE 29 — TWO PAGES ARE ONE COMPOSITION

# DESIGN THE SPREAD.
## PROTECT THE CENTER.

When a book is open, the reader sees both pages together.

The gutter can distort or hide information.

Avoid placing these directly across the center unless the composition deliberately accounts for binding:

- eyes and faces;
- small hands;
- important props;
- tiny text;
- precise character features.

Large background shapes can cross the gutter.

A face split down the nose usually should not.

## CHECK THE WHOLE SPREAD

[ ] Eye moves naturally across both pages  
[ ] Neither side becomes accidentally heavy  
[ ] Text and focal art do not compete  
[ ] Critical information avoids binding risk  
[ ] Composition survives realistic trim/binding

> **Build the pages flat first. Preview the open book second.**

Exact gutter and safe-area requirements depend on the actual printer, format, page count, paper, and binding.

### ART / LAYOUT
Use real center/gutter guide. No fake page curl, center shadow, or photographed-book mockup.

# PAGES 30–31 — MAKE THE PAGE TURN DO SOMETHING

# DON'T SPEND THE REVEAL BEFORE THE TURN.

A page turn can do at least three useful jobs.

## REVEAL

**Before:** anticipation or partial information.  
**After:** the thing itself.

A child hears something enormous outside.

Do not show it yet.

Turn the page.

Now show it.

## CONTRAST

**Before:** crowded, noisy, dark, tense.  
**After:** open, quiet, bright, relieved.

The physical turn strengthens the change.

## PUNCHLINE

**Before:** setup.  
**After:** visual answer, surprise, or joke.

Timing is part of the illustration.

## KEEP THE SETUP DISCIPLINED

AI may try to make the setup page impressive too.

Resist that.

If the next spread contains the reveal, the setup may need to be smaller, quieter, or incomplete.

> **What does the reader know now — and what are you saving for after the turn?**

If the answer is "everything," the reveal is already gone.

### ART / LAYOUT
Harvest only the strongest 2–3 reveal concepts. Give them room; do not preserve five tiny source panels.

# PAGES 32–33 — PAGE TURN: BEFORE & AFTER

# THE JOB OF THE PAGE CHANGED.

## BEFORE THE TURN

The manuscript creates anticipation.

Art direction:

- smaller composition;
- restrained detail;
- character looking toward the source;
- open space;
- answer withheld;
- visual intensity below the next spread.

The page should make the reader want to turn it.

## AFTER THE TURN

Now spend the visual budget.

Art direction:

- wider or more dominant composition;
- reveal fully readable;
- scale contrast clear;
- text placed where it does not weaken the reveal;
- detail concentrated on the story event.

## WHAT CHANGED?

Not merely the prompt.

The first page **withholds**.

The second **delivers**.

That is art direction across a sequence.

### ART / LAYOUT
Present as actual flat pages/spread, not a photograph of a physical book.
