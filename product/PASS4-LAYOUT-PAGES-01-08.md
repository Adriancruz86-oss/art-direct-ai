# PAGE 2 — HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE

# DON'T COPY A STYLE. BUILD A VISUAL LANGUAGE.

AI can make attractive pictures quickly.

This guide is about something harder: making a sequence of illustrations that feels **intentional, consistent, and specific to your book**.

You will learn to control five things:

**RESTRAINT**  
Decide what does *not* need detail.

**CHARACTER**  
Lock identity before the model redesigns your cast.

**PAGE DESIGN**  
Compose for words, pacing, gutters, and page turns.

**ITERATION**  
Diagnose failures instead of regenerating blindly.

**DIRECTION**  
Prompt with visual decisions—not piles of adjectives.

Throughout the guide, the boy and dog examples deliberately use a loose visual language:

> warm paper · graphite · colored pencil · light wash · sparse faces · simplified hair · plain clothing · unfinished areas

The point is not to copy that look.

The point is to see how **rules create a look**.

### ART / LAYOUT
Small samples of paper/mark behavior only. Avoid a grid of UI-like cards.

### ASSET
Harvest tiny non-character texture/mark crops from approved source artwork.

# PAGE 3 — STOP MAKING AI-LOOKING CHILDREN'S BOOKS

# AI ISN'T THE PROBLEM.

## DEFAULT AI IS.

A generator can give you glowing light, perfect skin, detailed hair, decorative backgrounds, cinematic color, and polished clothing in seconds.

That is exactly why so many results begin to feel interchangeable.

When you ask for a **"beautiful children's-book illustration,"** the model is free to make most of the visual decisions.

Your job is not to hide that AI was involved.

Your job is to stop accepting its first visual instincts as art direction.

> **Don't ask the model to make the best image it can. Ask it to make the image your book needs.**

### ART / LAYOUT
Opening art should dominate. Keep copy in one quiet text area.

### ASSET
Source comp #1 — KEEP ART. Re-typeset everything.

### LOCAL CHECK
Inspect tiny faces/hands at final reproduction size only. Repair locally if genuinely broken.

# PAGE 4 — WHY EVERYONE GETS THE SAME LOOK

# YOU ASKED FOR A SCENE.
## THE MODEL MADE THE REST OF THE DECISIONS.

Try:

> "A beautiful whimsical children's-book illustration."

You haven't decided:

- how much detail belongs in the face;
- whether hair is a shape or hundreds of strands;
- whether clothing stays plain;
- whether the background is suggested or rendered;
- whether empty paper remains empty;
- how dramatic the lighting becomes;
- what receives the reader's attention.

The model has to answer those questions somehow.

So it reaches for familiar signals of *beautiful*, *whimsical*, and *professional*.

**Too much finish.  
Too much detail.  
Too much lighting.  
Too much decoration.**

Before generating, ask:

> **What decisions would annoy me if the model made them for me?**

Those decisions belong in your direction.

### ART / LAYOUT
One strong problem visual, not a collage.

### ASSET
Source comp #2 — KEEP ART / LOCAL CLEANUP. Replace all sign/text content with real typography. Crowd remains secondary.

# PAGE 5 — YOU'RE THE ART DIRECTOR NOW

# A PROMPT NAMES THE SCENE.
## ART DIRECTION CONTROLS WHAT IT BECOMES.

Manuscript beat:

> A boy and his dog sit at the end of a dock at sunset.

That does **not** tell us whether the page needs a spectacular sky, detailed reflections, hoodie folds, glossy eyes, rim light, birds, flowers, or a perfectly rendered horizon.

Those are visual decisions.

An art director decides:

**WHAT MATTERS**  
The boy and dog sharing a quiet moment.

**WHAT STAYS SIMPLE**  
Clothing, shoreline, water, sky.

**WHAT REMAINS EMPTY**  
Raw paper and space for words.

**WHAT CANNOT DRIFT**  
Age, proportions, hair, outfit, dog markings, medium.

**WHAT IS PROHIBITED**  
Cinematic glow, ornate sky, decorative foliage, glossy rendering.

> **Generate less blindly. Decide more deliberately.**

### ART / LAYOUT
Use restrained dock imagery with generous paper. Avoid equal-size boxes; use one main illustration and short margin notes.

### ASSET
Approved restrained boy/dog dock imagery. Do not generate a new scene.

# PAGES 6–7 — DIAGNOSE YOUR ILLUSTRATION

# DON'T REGENERATE "BECAUSE IT LOOKS AI."

## NAME THE PROBLEM.

### 1 — TOO MUCH DETAIL
Hair, fabric, foliage, props, and textures all compete.

**Repair:** lower the detail ceiling.

### 2 — OVER-RENDERED FACES
Glossy eyes, smooth skin, perfect gradients, lashes, and cosmetic-looking blush overpower the page.

**Repair:** reduce the face to the marks the expression needs.

### 3 — CHARACTER DRIFT
Age, proportions, hair, clothing, or companion design changes between pages.

**Repair:** compare against the canonical reference and model pages.

### 4 — DECORATIVE CLUTTER
Empty areas fill with flowers, stars, pillows, plants, particles, or props.

**Repair:** protect negative space explicitly.

### 5 — STYLE DRIFT
Loose pencil becomes digital linework; light wash becomes airbrushed rendering.

**Repair:** restore the rendering rules instead of rewriting the scene.

## THE ONE-SENTENCE RULE

Before you regenerate, complete:

> # THE IMAGE FAILS BECAUSE __________.

If the answer is only **"I don't like it,"** you are not ready to regenerate.

# PAGE 8 — PART II OPENER / THE DETAIL BUDGET

# REMOVE THE AI FINGERPRINTS

## GIVE THE PAGE A DETAIL BUDGET.

AI does not get tired.

A human illustrator naturally creates hierarchy: time goes into the expression that matters; a sweater gets a few folds; a tree may be only a handful of marks; part of the paper stays untouched.

A generator can render **everything**.

The problem is not detail.

The problem is **detail without priority**.

Before generating, decide where the reader should look first.

Spend visual information there.

Simplify what supports it.

> **If a human illustrator had two hours for this page, where would those two hours go?**
