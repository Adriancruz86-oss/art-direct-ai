# PAGE 34 — STOP REGENERATING EVERYTHING

# ONE BAD HAND DOESN'T MEAN YOU HAVE A BAD PAGE.

Suppose the composition works.

The character looks right.  
The expression works.  
The background is restrained.  
There is room for text.

But one hand is malformed.

You regenerate the entire image.

Now the hand is better — but the face changed, the hoodie gained a zipper, the dog is larger, the background is busier, and your text space disappeared.

You fixed one problem by reopening ten decisions.

## SEPARATE WORKING FROM BROKEN

**WORKING**  
Scene · composition · character · expression · medium · palette · background · text space

**BROKEN**  
One hand · one shoe · one prop · one patch of detail · one drifting feature

> **Keep every correct decision you can. Reopen only the decisions that failed.**

### ART / LAYOUT
One successful page with a single visibly circled/localized defect. Avoid a dashboard.

### ASSET
Reuse retained/approved art with a known local defect example. No new scene generation.

# PAGES 35–36 — FIX IT IN EDITING

# HOW SMALL CAN THE FIX BE?

Not every defect is an art-direction failure.

Sometimes the image is simply broken:

- extra finger;
- merged hand and object;
- doubled shoe;
- impossible leg;
- malformed ear;
- duplicate prop;
- accidental symbol;
- nonsense texture.

If the rest of the illustration works, treat the problem as a **local production defect**.

## REPAIR THE SMALLEST USEFUL AREA

**Bad hand**  
Keep the character, pose, composition, and page. Repair the hand.

**Wrong shoe**  
Keep the body and clothing. Correct the shoe against the reference.

**Dog markings drifted**  
Keep the pose. Restore the canonical markings.

**Background too busy**  
Simplify the background without redesigning the characters.

**Hair over-rendered**  
Reduce internal strands while preserving the silhouette.

## DON'T "IMPROVE" EVERYTHING DURING A REPAIR

If the hand is wrong, don't also request prettier lighting, richer color, a better background, and more expression.

That isn't a repair.

It's another regeneration disguised as one.

## PREVENT WHAT YOU CAN

Complex walking and running poses are common failure points.

If movement does not matter to the story, a clear standing, seated, kneeling, or leaning pose may communicate the beat with less anatomy risk.

> **Movement should serve the manuscript — not prove the generator can draw movement.**

### ART / LAYOUT
Use source #21 repair concept. Make the defect unmistakable and label it as intentionally broken.

### ASSET
Source #21 — KEEP ART / real labels. Local repair examples only.

# PAGE 37 — CHANGE ONE VARIABLE AT A TIME

# DIAGNOSE → CHANGE → COMPARE.

When several things feel wrong, don't automatically rewrite the whole prompt.

Identify the dominant failure first.

**CHARACTER DRIFT?**  
Strengthen references and immutable features.

**TOO POLISHED?**  
Lower the rendering ceiling; restore raw paper and sparse marks.

**BACKGROUND TOO BUSY?**  
Reduce shapes, props, foliage, and texture.

**COMPOSITION FAILS?**  
Change placement, camera, focal hierarchy, or text-safe area.

**EMOTION TOO STRONG?**  
Reduce expression intensity, gesture, and facial rendering.

## CONTROLLED ITERATION

**1. Generate**  
**2. Name the failure**  
**3. Identify the responsible variable**  
**4. Preserve what works**  
**5. Repair/reopen only what failed**  
**6. Compare with references/model pages**  
**7. Repeat only for a specific remaining failure**

> **Random regeneration creates options. Controlled iteration creates a book.**

### ART / LAYOUT
Simple vertical process with one small worked image, not seven equal UI cards.

### ASSET
Reuse canonical/dock material.

# PAGES 38–39 — PROMPT FOR DECISIONS

# A LONG PROMPT ISN'T NECESSARILY A DIRECTED PROMPT.

Build direction in this order:

## 1 — SUBJECT
Who or what must appear?

## 2 — ACTION
What is physically happening?

## 3 — COMPOSITION
Camera distance, placement, focal point, text-safe area.

## 4 — CHARACTER LOCKS
What must match established references?

## 5 — MEDIUM + SURFACE
How should the marks and material behave?

## 6 — DETAIL CEILING
Where is rendering allowed? What stays simple?

## 7 — MOOD
What emotional temperature belongs here?

## 8 — RESTRAINT
Which model defaults would damage the page?

### MINI EXAMPLE

**Scene:** boy and dog on dock.

**Composition:** low/right, open upper-left for text.

**Locks:** canonical boy/dog.

**Medium:** loose graphite + colored pencil + light wash on warm paper.

**Detail:** sparse faces, broad shoreline, minimal reflections.

**Restraint:** no cinematic glow, ornate sky, glossy rendering, decorative foliage.

> **The hierarchy is a thinking tool — not a requirement to write a 500-word prompt.**

Use one flowing vertical hierarchy across the spread, with the mini-example integrated beside it. Avoid an eight-box dashboard.

# PAGE 42 — NEGATIVE DIRECTION + PROMPT BUILDER

# WHAT DOES YOUR BOOK REFUSE TO DO?

"Don't make it look AI" is not visual direction.

Name observable behaviors:

> No glossy eyes. No rim light. No individual hair strands. No decorative foreground plants. No ornate fabric. No cinematic sunset. Don't fill open paper merely to make it look finished.

Negative direction is **project-specific**.

A magical book may need glow. A graphic book may need clean digital edges. An ornate costume may matter to the story.

Build a refusal list for *your* visual language.

## PROMPT BUILDER

**SUBJECT** — Who/what?  
**ACTION** — What happens?  
**COMPOSITION** — Camera, placement, text space?  
**CHARACTER LOCKS** — What must match?  
**MEDIUM + SURFACE** — How do marks behave?  
**DETAIL CEILING** — What gets rendered?  
**MOOD** — Emotional temperature?  
**RESTRAINT** — What must not appear?

### FINAL CHECK

> **Have I directed the decisions that matter — or mostly described something I hope will look pretty?**

### ART / LAYOUT
Working-sheet feel with ample writable/visual breathing room. Do not turn into dense form UI.
