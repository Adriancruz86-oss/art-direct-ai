# 5 — The Detail Budget

AI does not get tired.

That sounds like an advantage until it starts rendering things nobody needed rendered.

A human illustrator has limited time and attention. That naturally creates hierarchy. They may spend time on the child's expression, suggest the sweater with three folds, indicate a tree with a few marks, and leave part of the paper untouched.

A generator can give the sweater twelve folds, every leaf an edge, every curl a highlight, every brick a texture, and every empty corner another object.

The problem is not detail itself.

The problem is **detail without priority**.

## Give each page a detail budget

Before generating, decide where the reader should look first.

Spend the most visual information there.

Then deliberately reduce everything that supports it.

If the emotional beat is the child's face, the background does not need to compete.

If the enormous tree is the reveal, the child's shirt does not need embroidery.

If the page is about a tiny object in the child's hand, the room can nearly disappear.

Ask:

> **If a human illustrator had two hours for this page, where would those two hours go?**

Then direct the model as though those hours still cost something.

## Three useful levels

**Too much**  
Several areas compete for attention. Surfaces are rendered because they can be.

**Just right**  
The focal idea gets enough information to read clearly. Supporting areas are simplified.

**Too little**  
Important forms, emotion, or story information are missing.

Restraint does not mean unfinished work.

It means knowing **what deserves finishing**.

# 6 — Background Detail

A background has a job.

Usually it needs to establish place, support mood, provide useful story information, and help the main action read.

It does not need to prove that the generator knows what leaves look like.

## The common failure

Ask for a child beside a lake and the model may give you:

- individual reeds;
- layered mountains;
- reflected clouds;
- dozens of trees;
- rocks along the shoreline;
- flowers near the foreground;
- birds in the sky;
- texture across every inch of water.

The result can be beautiful.

It can also turn a quiet moment into a landscape demonstration.

## Direct the background by function

Instead of:

> Beautiful detailed lakeside background.

Try:

> Distant shoreline suggested with two or three broad shapes. Water mostly open wash and visible paper. Only a few marks near the dock. No decorative foreground plants. Sky quiet and secondary to the characters.

Notice what changed.

You did not merely ask for "less detail."

You told the model **where detail is allowed to stop**.

## Protect empty paper

If the composition needs quiet space, say so explicitly:

> Preserve visible warm paper in the upper half. Do not fill the open area with clouds, birds, foliage, texture, particles, or decorative objects.

Empty space is not a generation failure.

It is part of the composition.

### Background test

Cover the characters with your hand.

Does the background still look like it is trying to win an illustration award by itself?

If so, it may be doing too much.

# 7 — Fabric & Clothing Detail

Fabric is one of the easiest places for AI to spend detail nobody requested.

A plain dress becomes patterned.

A hoodie gains stitching, seams, drawstring hardware, realistic folds and fabric texture.

A sweater develops a visible knit.

None of those things is automatically wrong.

But ask:

> **Does the story care?**

If not, you are paying visual attention for no narrative return.

## Clothing usually has three jobs

1. Help identify the character.
2. Explain the pose.
3. Contribute a useful shape or color.

That is enough for most pages.

## Pattern creates visual debt

A generator can invent an elaborate fabric pattern instantly.

But once you approve it, that pattern becomes something the book may need to reproduce consistently from angle to angle and page to page.

That turns decoration into a continuity problem.

Use boring instructions when boring is correct:

> Plain red hoodie. No logo, printed pattern, decorative stitching, fabric texture, or extra trim. Only enough folds to explain the pose.

That is strong art direction.

## Folds should explain form

Keep the folds that tell us:

- an elbow is bent;
- the character is sitting;
- the torso is twisting;
- fabric hangs over a knee.

Remove folds whose only purpose is to demonstrate realistic cloth simulation.

### The fabric test

Would a traditional illustrator redraw this much clothing detail on twenty pages?

If probably not, simplify it before it becomes part of the character design.

# 8 — Faces Are Where AI Gives Itself Away

A loose illustration can suddenly look synthetic because of one highly finished face.

The model may give you:

- perfectly smooth skin;
- glossy catchlights;
- symmetrical eyes;
- individual lashes;
- carefully graded cheeks;
- shiny lips;
- detailed teeth;
- polished hair framing a softly lit face.

None of those features is inherently bad.

Together, they can belong to a completely different visual language than the rest of the page.

## Describe how much face is allowed

Do not only describe who the character is.

Describe the rendering ceiling:

> Facial features indicated with very few pencil marks. Simple eyes without glossy highlights. Skin mostly flat with a faint wash. No individual eyelashes. Mouth as a simple mark. Preserve small hand-drawn asymmetries.

The goal is not to make the child less expressive.

It is to stop using rendering as a substitute for expression.

A head tilt, eyebrow angle, small mouth change, posture, or direction of gaze may carry the entire emotion.

## Watch the word "cute"

Words such as *cute*, *adorable*, *beautiful*, and *charming* can encourage:

- larger eyes;
- rounder cheeks;
- smaller noses;
- sweeter smiles;
- cleaner symmetry;
- more polished rendering.

If that is not your character design, describe the actual design instead of asking for cuteness.

## The thumbnail test

Shrink the page until the face is close to its likely printed size.

Does the expression still read?

If yes, detail visible only at 300-percent zoom probably does not need to be there.

# 9 — The Power of the Quiet Page

AI wants to fill.

You do not have to let it.

One of the strongest decisions in a children's book can be a page where almost nothing happens visually.

A small character.

One meaningful object.

A few pencil marks.

A large area of untouched paper.

That quiet can communicate loneliness, relief, waiting, wonder, distance, uncertainty, or simply a pause before the next event.

## Empty does not mean unfinished

When a generator sees open space, it often tries to help.

A blank wall gets pictures.

An empty field gets flowers.

A quiet sky gets clouds and birds.

A dark room gets decorative light.

A simple desk gets books, pencils, plants and toys.

Every addition may be plausible.

Together, they destroy the pause.

## Direct the absence

Try instructions such as:

> Keep most of the page as untouched warm paper.

> One small figure low on the page. No decorative background objects.

> Allow the watercolor wash to fade completely into raw paper.

> Do not balance the empty side with another object.

That last instruction matters.

Design does not always need to feel evenly filled.

## Use quiet strategically

A quiet page becomes more powerful when it follows something busy.

A close-up becomes more powerful after a distant view.

A full spread becomes more powerful after a small spot illustration.

This is why art direction cannot happen one generated image at a time.

You are building a sequence.

### The restraint rule

Before approving a page, ask:

> **What can I remove without losing the story?**

Remove it mentally.

If the page gets stronger, it probably did not belong there.
