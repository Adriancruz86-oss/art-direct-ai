# Part III — The AI Fingerprints

## Chapter 11 — The everything-is-important problem

A strong illustration has hierarchy.

Something matters most. Something supports it. Something barely needs to be there at all.

Generated images often flatten that hierarchy by giving everything the same level of attention.

The child's face is detailed.

The wallpaper is detailed.

The bookshelf is detailed.

Every book on the shelf has a visible spine.

Every leaf outside the window is distinct.

The rug has a pattern.

The mug has decoration.

The lamp has texture.

The background may be technically impressive, but the reader's eye has nowhere obvious to land.

### Detail is a spotlight

Every extra mark says, “Look here.”

If you place the same spotlight on every object, you no longer have a focal point.

Traditional illustrators routinely simplify secondary information because drawing time is finite and visual attention is limited.

A generator has no fatigue. It can keep going.

That means you must create the hierarchy on purpose.

### Assign three levels before generating

For each page, divide the scene into:

**PRIMARY** — the story beat.

This receives the clearest silhouette, strongest contrast, most useful expression, or most deliberate detail.

**SECONDARY** — information needed to understand the moment.

This should read clearly but should not compete.

**SUPPORTING** — atmosphere, setting, or objects that can remain suggested.

Example:

A child is secretly feeding a stray cat under the kitchen table.

Primary: child and cat interaction.

Secondary: table legs and bowl so the hiding place makes sense.

Supporting: the rest of the kitchen.

The supporting kitchen does not need twelve labeled jars, patterned curtains, an herb garden, copper pans, a fruit bowl, a tiled backsplash, and morning light sparkling through every glass.

### Blur is not the only way to simplify

Do not assume the background must be physically out of focus.

Illustrative simplification can come from:

- fewer marks;
- flatter color;
- lower contrast;
- broader shapes;
- partial outlines;
- unpainted paper;
- omitted objects.

This is often more faithful to a handmade picture-book style than photographic depth of field.

### The squint test

Squint at the image or shrink it down.

What do you notice first?

If the answer is “the patterned curtain,” but the page is about the child's worried expression, the hierarchy is wrong.

### A useful direction

> The child and cat are the only fully resolved elements. Furniture is simplified into broad shapes. Background objects are sparse and low-contrast. Do not add decorative objects to empty surfaces. Preserve quiet paper and visual hierarchy.

The point is not to make the image empty.

The point is to decide what deserves to exist loudly.

## Chapter 12 — The glow problem

Generative models love glow.

Give them words like *magical*, *wonder*, *mysterious*, *dreamlike*, *hopeful*, or *fantasy*, and suddenly everything starts emitting light.

Books glow.

Fireflies glow like LEDs.

Windows glow.

Hair has rim light.

Dust sparkles.

Ordinary toys become sacred artifacts.

A little glow can be beautiful.

The problem is when glow becomes a substitute for visual storytelling.

### Light should come from somewhere

Before accepting a luminous effect, ask:

**What is the light source?**

If the answer is clear — candle, flashlight, moon, lamp, magical object that truly emits light — then the effect may belong.

If the answer is “because the scene is emotional,” you may be looking at visual inflation.

### Magic does not require particles

A magical moment can be expressed through:

- scale;
- silence;
- color contrast;
- unusual composition;
- a single impossible object;
- the character's reaction;
- negative space.

You do not automatically need:

- glowing dust;
- sparkles;
- bokeh circles;
- floating motes;
- rim lighting around every character;
- shafts of volumetric light.

The more often you use those effects, the less magical they feel.

### Protect ordinary scenes from cinematic lighting

A child eating cereal at 7:00 a.m. usually does not need a golden beam of light wrapping around the face like a movie poster.

A useful constraint:

> Soft ordinary room light. No dramatic rim lighting, no volumetric beams, no glowing edges, no floating particles.

That direction can do more for a handmade look than adding five extra medium adjectives.

### Reserve the strongest effect

If the book truly contains one glowing magical object, protect it.

Do not let unrelated pages use the same visual language.

The one real glow should feel special because the rest of the book does not glow.

That is visual storytelling.

## Chapter 13 — Decorative clutter

The model sees an empty shelf and wants to fill it.

It sees a blank wall and adds framed art.

It sees a field and adds flowers.

It sees a desk and adds pencils, books, a plant, a mug, notes, a lamp, and maybe a small toy.

It is trying to make the image feel complete.

But completion is not always the goal.

### Every invented object creates a continuity debt

If a red lamp appears in the child's bedroom on page three, readers may reasonably expect that lamp to exist again later.

If a framed rabbit picture appears behind the bed, that becomes part of the room.

If the kitchen has blue cabinets in one scene and green cabinets in another, the model's harmless decoration has become visible drift.

The more objects you let it invent, the more continuity you must manage.

### Set dressing is a decision

Before generating a recurring location, decide a small number of anchor objects.

For a child's bedroom, perhaps:

- low wooden bed;
- blue blanket;
- small white nightstand;
- one red lamp;
- two framed drawings;
- worn rug.

Those become the room's identity.

Everything else can remain quiet.

### “Cozy” can be dangerous

Words like *cozy*, *whimsical*, *charming*, and *storybook* often summon decorative shorthand:

- string lights;
- patterned pillows;
- plants;
- framed prints;
- candles;
- blankets;
- tiny trinkets;
- flowers.

If those objects serve the story, keep them.

If they are only there because “cozy” demanded proof, simplify.

### Empty surfaces are allowed

A plain wall can be a plain wall.

A table can hold one bowl.

A field can be grass.

A child's shirt can be blank.

This sounds obvious until you compare it with how often generated scenes decorate every available surface.

### The object audit

After generating, count the objects that were not requested.

Then ask of each one:

1. Does it support the story?
2. Does it establish a recurring location?
3. Does it improve composition?
4. Will I now have to remember it later?

If it fails all four questions, consider removing it.

## Chapter 14 — The same-face problem

There are two different consistency failures.

The first is one character changing from page to page.

The second is every character looking like the same person with different hair.

Generative systems can fall into both.

### Identity is more than hair color

If three children share the same:

- eye shape;
- nose proportion;
- cheek structure;
- mouth shape;
- head width;
- smile;
- eyebrow placement;

then changing hair and clothing may not be enough.

You have created palette swaps, not a cast.

### Build contrast between recurring characters

Design characters relationally.

If one child has a narrow face, perhaps another has a wider face.

If one has a very round hair silhouette, another may have a taller or flatter silhouette.

If one has compact proportions, another may be lankier.

If one character's expressions are broad, another may be quieter.

You are not trying to exaggerate stereotypes.

You are creating readable design differences.

### Avoid describing everyone as attractive

Repeated adjectives like *cute*, *beautiful*, *handsome*, *adorable*, and *charming* can pull multiple characters toward the same aesthetic center.

Describe actual visual structure instead.

### Make a cast sheet

Place recurring characters together in one neutral reference.

Check:

- relative heights;
- head shapes;
- hair silhouettes;
- shoulder widths;
- age differences;
- skin tones;
- clothing silhouettes;
- facial economy.

If the cast does not read as distinct when shown together, fix that before generating twenty pages.

### Consistency should preserve difference

The goal is not to make every face perfectly repeatable by forcing it toward a generic template.

The goal is to make each character reliably themselves.

# Part IV — Build a Book, Not a Folder of Pictures

## Chapter 15 — A repeatable page workflow

The easiest way to lose control of a book is to treat each page as a fresh creative experiment.

A repeatable workflow reduces that drift.

Here is a practical page-by-page system.

### Step 1 — Read the manuscript line without thinking about style

Identify the actual story beat.

What changes on this page?

What does the reader need to understand or feel?

Do not begin with “What would look cool?”

Begin with “What matters?”

### Step 2 — Choose the page's job

Is this page primarily:

- establishing location;
- showing action;
- showing emotion;
- delivering a reveal;
- creating quiet;
- showing an object;
- connecting two larger moments?

The job determines the composition.

### Step 3 — Choose the camera distance

Do not default to medium portrait distance.

Choose intentionally:

- very wide;
- wide;
- medium;
- close-up;
- detail/insert.

Check the two previous pages so you do not repeat the same distance mechanically.

### Step 4 — Reserve text space

Mark where typography will live before you generate.

Treat that area as part of the composition, not leftover room.

### Step 5 — Load continuity

Bring forward only what must remain stable:

- character reference;
- wardrobe;
- recurring location anchors;
- prop design;
- visual-language rules.

Do not rewrite these from memory on every page.

### Step 6 — Set the detail budget

Name the primary element.

Then state what stays simple.

Example:

> Primary attention on the child's hands and the broken toy. Face simple. Shirt plain. Floor suggested with a light wash. Background furniture only as broad shapes.

### Step 7 — Add prohibitions relevant to this page

Do not paste a hundred negatives automatically.

Use the ones that solve likely failure modes.

A magical forest may need “no glowing particles.”

A bedroom may need “do not add decorative wall art.”

A character close-up may need “no glossy eyes or detailed eyelashes.”

### Step 8 — Generate, then diagnose

Do not immediately ask for another variation.

Complete the sentence:

> This result fails because ________.

Then change the smallest relevant layer.

### Step 9 — Choose edit or regenerate

If 90 percent works, preserve it.

Do not destroy a successful composition because one hand is wrong.

### Step 10 — Compare against neighboring pages

Place the new image beside the previous two.

Ask:

- same character?
- same medium?
- same detail ceiling?
- enough rhythm?
- accidental repeated composition?
- any new object that creates continuity debt?

Only then approve the page.

### Create an approval status

Use a simple production label:

**ROUGH** — composition only.

**CANDIDATE** — potentially usable but not fully checked.

**LOCKED** — approved for continuity and style.

**FINAL** — edited, proofed, and placed in layout.

This prevents “I think this was the one” chaos after thirty generations.

## Chapter 16 — Editing versus regenerating

One of the biggest efficiency gains in AI-assisted illustration comes from knowing when to stop generating.

Regeneration is seductive because it feels effortless.

But every new generation can disturb things that were already correct.

You fix the hand and lose the face.

You fix the face and the room changes.

You fix the room and the clothing becomes ornate.

You fix the clothing and the composition shifts.

At some point, regeneration becomes more expensive than editing.

### Edit when the problem is local

Good edit candidates include:

- one incorrect hand;
- one extra object;
- a small clothing artifact;
- a background symbol;
- one wrong eye highlight;
- a distracting flower cluster;
- an accidental pattern;
- a color that drifted;
- a small anatomy defect;
- text-safe space that needs cleaning.

If the rest of the image works, preserve it.

### Regenerate when the problem is structural

Regeneration makes more sense when:

- the entire composition is wrong;
- the camera distance is wrong;
- character identity is badly lost;
- the medium is completely wrong;
- the emotional beat is wrong;
- the perspective makes the scene unusable;
- the page has no viable text space;
- hierarchy is fundamentally broken.

### The 80-percent rule

If roughly 80 percent or more of the image is doing its job, strongly consider editing before regenerating.

This is not a mathematical law.

It is a habit that protects good accidents.

### Preserve successful weirdness

Traditional art contains irregularities.

A slightly awkward pencil curve, uneven wash edge, asymmetrical eyebrow, or imperfect contour can make an illustration feel alive.

Do not clean every irregularity merely because editing tools make it possible.

Fix defects.

Do not sterilize personality.

### Keep an edit log

For each final page, record major corrections:

- removed decorative pattern;
- simplified hair;
- restored jacket color;
- cleared text area;
- corrected hand;
- reduced background contrast.

This list becomes useful later because repeated edits reveal weaknesses in your prompt system.

If you remove floral fabric patterns on eight pages, the lesson is not “keep editing flowers.”

The lesson is “strengthen the wardrobe constraint upstream.”

## Chapter 17 — The human pass

A finished AI-assisted illustration should still receive a human pass.

Not because every pixel needs manual repainting.

Because the final responsibility for the page belongs to you.

### Check storytelling before polish

First ask:

- Is the story beat readable?
- Does the character look at the right thing?
- Is the emotion calibrated correctly?
- Does the page create the intended pacing?
- Is the reader's eye going where it should?

Do this before obsessing over tiny artifacts.

### Check continuity

Compare against locked references and neighboring pages.

Look for:

- wardrobe drift;
- age drift;
- hair-length drift;
- changing props;
- room-layout changes;
- color changes;
- medium changes;
- inconsistent level of facial detail.

### Check the “AI tells” at print size

Do not only inspect at 400 percent zoom.

View at the approximate physical size the reader will see.

Then look for:

- over-detailed hair;
- over-rendered eyes;
- impossible fabric patterns;
- decorative clutter;
- random tiny symbols;
- text-like marks;
- repeated meaningless shapes;
- overly perfect gradients;
- strange hands or merged objects.

### Make selective hand marks if they help

A few real pencil strokes, erased edges, painted corrections, texture passes, or intentional rough marks can unify a page.

But do not add fake “handmade” damage everywhere as camouflage.

The point is not to trick someone into believing a provenance story.

The point is to make the art better.

### Crop with intent

Cropping is art direction.

A generation that feels weak at full frame may become excellent when cropped tighter.

Cropping can:

- remove clutter;
- strengthen hierarchy;
- create text space;
- make action feel closer;
- restore asymmetry;
- remove a defective edge element.

Do not treat the generated canvas boundary as sacred.

### Typography is part of the illustration

Before final approval, place the actual text.

A page is not finished when the image is finished.

Check:

- contrast behind type;
- comfortable margins;
- gutter safety;
- line length;
- whether illustration marks point awkwardly into letters;
- whether text placement changes the visual balance.

A good illustration can become a bad spread after type is added.

### The final question

Before locking a page, ask:

**If I could not tell anyone how this image was made, would I still be proud of the decisions on the page?**

That is the standard.

## Chapter 18 — The complete worked example

This chapter combines the system into one page from start to finish.

We will use a deliberately ordinary manuscript line because ordinary scenes expose over-rendering quickly.

### Manuscript line

> Maya sat beneath the kitchen table and slid half her sandwich toward the wet orange cat.

The story beat is not “beautiful kitchen.”

It is secret kindness.

That immediately tells us what matters.

### First-pass novice prompt

> Cute children's-book watercolor illustration of a little girl named Maya sitting under a cozy kitchen table feeding half a sandwich to an adorable wet orange cat. Beautiful, whimsical, detailed, warm morning light, professional children's-book art.

This prompt sounds reasonable.

It also gives the model permission to do almost everything we have spent this book learning to control.

### Likely first-result problems

A typical polished output may include:

- oversized glossy eyes;
- beautifully rendered wet fur;
- detailed wood grain;
- patterned socks or dress;
- a charming kitchen full of plants and jars;
- golden light beams;
- cute decorative clutter;
- perfectly arranged crumbs;
- flowers or patterned curtains;
- an expressive cat face that reads like animation;
- no useful text-safe space.

The image might be attractive.

It might also be wrong for the book.

### Diagnosis

Complete the sentence:

> The image fails because the quiet secret interaction is buried under beautification, background detail, and cinematic polish.

That diagnosis tells us what to fix.

### Visual-language decisions

For this example, assume the book uses:

- warm off-white paper;
- loose graphite contour;
- restrained colored pencil;
- thin watercolor washes;
- visible unpainted paper;
- simplified faces;
- plain clothing;
- very little interior background detail.

### Character decisions

Maya:

- seven years old;
- compact proportions;
- dark bob ending at jaw;
- simple side fringe;
- plain mustard-yellow short-sleeved dress;
- red socks;
- sparse facial marks;
- no glossy eyes.

Cat:

- thin orange stray;
- slightly oversized ears;
- wet fur suggested with a few directional marks, not strand-by-strand rendering;
- no cartoon smile.

### Composition decision

The emotional action is low to the ground.

Place the viewer near floor level.

Maya sits in the lower-right beneath the table. The cat enters from lower-left. Their hands/paws and the sandwich form the focal triangle.

Keep the upper-left area mostly quiet for text.

Show only enough table and chair structure to explain the hiding place.

### Art-directed prompt

**SCENE**

> Maya, age seven, sits cross-legged under a kitchen table and quietly slides half a sandwich toward a thin wet orange stray cat. The cat is cautious, not cartoonishly happy. Maya is gentle and slightly nervous about being discovered.

**COMPOSITION**

> Low floor-level view. Maya low on the right, cat low on the left, sandwich between them as the focal connection. Keep the upper-left third mostly open warm paper for text. Show only simple table legs and one chair; do not fully describe the kitchen.

**VISUAL LANGUAGE**

> Loose graphite and restrained colored pencil with thin watercolor washes on warm off-white paper. Broken imperfect edges. Visible paper. Faces described with very few marks. Broad simple shapes rather than polished rendering.

**CONTINUITY**

> Maya keeps her established jaw-length dark bob, compact seven-year-old proportions, plain mustard-yellow short-sleeved dress, and red socks.

**CONSTRAINTS**

> Plain dress: no pattern, lace, embroidery, trim, or fabric texture emphasis. Hair as one dark mass with only a few directional strokes. Cat fur simplified into grouped wet shapes, not individual strands. No glossy eyes. No golden rim light. No floating particles. No decorative kitchen objects. No plants, wall art, patterned curtains, or styled countertop. Preserve open paper.

### What changed?

Notice that the improved direction does not merely add adjectives.

It removes choices.

The first prompt asked the model to make something beautiful.

The second tells the model what the page is for.

### After generation: the edit pass

Suppose the improved output is strong but still includes:

- a small flower pattern on one sock;
- three unnecessary jars in the distant background;
- a slightly too-bright eye highlight on the cat.

Do not regenerate the entire page.

Edit those three problems.

Then place the actual manuscript text in the upper-left area.

### Final evaluation

Run the restraint test:

1. Can anything disappear without hurting the story?
2. Is the interaction still the first thing we notice?
3. Does Maya match her reference?
4. Is the cat believable without being over-rendered?
5. Does the page leave enough quiet space?
6. Does it belong beside the surrounding spreads?
7. Would a human illustrator realistically spend the represented amount of effort on each part of this image?

If yes, lock the page.

That is the workflow.

Not “prompt until something pretty appears.”

**Decide. Generate. Diagnose. Constrain. Edit. Compare. Lock.**

# Appendix C — The one-page production card

For each spread, fill this out before generating final art.

**STORY BEAT:**

What changes or matters here?

**PAGE JOB:**

Establishing / action / emotion / reveal / quiet / transition / object.

**PRIMARY FOCUS:**

What deserves the detail budget?

**TEXT AREA:**

Where must remain quiet?

**CAMERA DISTANCE:**

Very wide / wide / medium / close / detail.

**CONTINUITY LOCKS:**

Character, wardrobe, location, props.

**SIMPLIFY:**

What should remain broad, unfinished, flat, or omitted?

**PROHIBITIONS:**

Which predictable AI habits are most likely to hurt this page?

**AFTER GENERATION:**

The image fails because ______________________________.

**ACTION:**

Accept / edit / regenerate.

**STATUS:**

Rough / candidate / locked / final.

# Closing — The goal is not to hide the tool

There is a temptation to frame all of this as a way to make AI art harder to detect.

That is too small a goal.

The real goal is to stop accepting generic creative decisions simply because they arrived instantly.

A camera can produce an image instantly. That does not make every photograph good.

A digital brush can create a perfect line instantly. That does not make every drawing thoughtful.

Generative AI can create enormous amounts of visual material quickly. That speed does not replace taste.

It makes taste more important.

Your job is not to prove that the machine can draw.

Everyone already knows it can draw.

Your job is to decide what deserves to be drawn, what should stay simple, what should repeat, what should disappear, where the reader should look, and when the image is finished.

That is art direction.

And when the model gives you more than the book needs, remember the simplest instruction in this entire guide:

**Take something away.**
