# 10 — Decide What Cannot Change

Character consistency does not begin when page two looks wrong.

It begins before page one.

A generator can reproduce a general idea — brown-haired boy, red hoodie, spotted dog — while quietly changing the actual character every time.

The face gets older.
The head gets narrower.
The hair grows.
The hoodie changes shape.
The shoes become boots.
The dog gets taller.
The spots move.
The illustration still looks plausible, so the drift can sneak through one page at a time.

By page ten, you may have ten cousins instead of one character.

## Lock identity before poses

Do not begin with eye color and freckles.

Begin with what the reader recognizes from across the room:

- apparent age;
- head-to-body proportion;
- overall silhouette;
- hair silhouette and length;
- face shape;
- relative height;
- core clothing silhouette;
- companion size and shape.

Tiny matching details cannot rescue a silhouette that has changed.

## Divide features into three groups

### IMMUTABLE
These define identity and should almost never change:

- apparent age;
- skin tone;
- body proportions;
- face structure;
- hair color, length and primary silhouette;
- signature glasses, hearing aid, birthmark or other identity-defining feature;
- core outfit when the story uses one;
- recurring companion's size, markings and major features;
- relative height between recurring characters.

### SCENE-DEPENDENT
These change because the story requires them:

- pose;
- expression;
- direction of gaze;
- temporary outerwear;
- story props;
- lighting response;
- dirt, rain, sleepiness or other temporary conditions.

### FLEXIBLE
These can vary slightly without changing identity:

- exact placement of a loose hair mark;
- minor clothing folds;
- hand position within a similar pose;
- small pencil irregularities;
- tiny asymmetries.

The goal is not to freeze the character.

It is to know what is allowed to move.

## Hair is a shape before it is hair

"Brown messy hair" is not a strong continuity instruction.

Try:

> Medium-brown hair forming one loose rounded mass, slightly wider above the ears, short at the neck, simple uneven fringe. Only a few internal pencil marks.

That protects both identity and restraint.

## Clothing is part of the silhouette

If the character normally wears a red hoodie, define enough to repeat it:

- hood shape;
- sleeve length;
- body length;
- plain or patterned;
- jeans/trousers shape;
- shoe type.

Then stop.

Do not accidentally turn a continuity note into a fashion specification.

### Page-one test

Before finished pages begin, you should be able to answer:

1. What does this character look like from across the room?
2. What five features cannot change?
3. What is the simplest version of the core outfit?
4. How much facial detail belongs in this book?
5. What does the character look like from the side?
6. What would count as unacceptable drift?

If those answers are still fuzzy, page one is too early.

# 11 — Build a Reference Sheet You Will Actually Use

A reference sheet is not decoration for your project folder.

It is a decision record.

Its job is to answer the questions that later generations are most likely to improvise.

## Start with the useful views

For most children's-book characters, you do not need twenty poses.

Start with:

- front;
- three-quarter;
- side;
- back if clothing/hair requires it;
- one neutral standing pose;
- one seated pose if the manuscript uses it frequently.

The views should teach identity, not trap the character into one pose.

## Add a small expression range

Show enough to establish the book's emotional ceiling:

- neutral;
- happy;
- worried/sad;
- surprised or angry only if the story genuinely needs it.

A quiet hand-drawn character should not suddenly become a giant open-mouth cartoon reaction because the prompt says "excited."

Consistency includes **how emotion is rendered**.

## Record the features that matter

Useful reference notes include:

**Hair** — silhouette, length, part/fringe, major mass.

**Face** — shape, feature spacing, amount of rendering.

**Body** — proportions and relative height.

**Clothing** — core silhouette and simple color relationships.

**Shoes** — type and basic shape. Shoes drift more often than people notice.

**Companion** — body size, muzzle, ears, tail, markings and collar/accessory.

**Palette** — a few stable color anchors, not twenty sampled micro-colors.

## Do not over-reference micro-detail

A reference sheet can become another form of AI overworking.

You usually do not need:

- ten eye close-ups;
- every possible hand pose;
- dozens of facial expressions;
- exact fabric textures;
- hundreds of hair marks;
- microscopic color variations.

Reference the things that preserve identity.

Leave ordinary drawing variation room to breathe.

# 12 — Consistency Is More Than the Face

Character drift is often subtle because creators compare faces first.

But identity can fail even when the face looks close.

Compare each new generation in layers.

## 1. Apparent age

Did the child suddenly look three years older or younger?

Watch head size, jaw shape, limb length, shoulder width and facial maturity.

## 2. Silhouette and proportions

Compare:
- head-to-body ratio;
- torso length;
- leg length;
- shoulder width;
- overall height;
- companion scale.

## 3. Hair

Compare the outer mass before internal strands:
- length;
- width;
- fringe;
- part;
- volume;
- relationship to ears, jaw and neck.

## 4. Face system

Do not demand pixel-identical features.

Check whether the same design language remains:
- face shape;
- eye spacing;
- nose treatment;
- mouth treatment;
- amount of facial detail;
- emotional rendering ceiling.

## 5. Clothing

Check:
- garment type;
- silhouette;
- sleeve/hem length;
- core colors;
- layers;
- logos/patterns that appeared without permission.

## 6. Shoes and small anchors

Shoes, collars, glasses, hair clips and other small anchors are easy to overlook and easy for the model to redesign.

## 7. Companion design

For recurring animals, compare:
- size;
- muzzle length;
- ear shape;
- tail;
- coat length;
- marking placement;
- collar/accessory.

### The drift rule

If you need to explain why two versions are "technically the same character," the design has probably drifted too far.

The reader should recognize them before reading your explanation.

# 13 — Catch Drift Before It Spreads

Do not wait until the book is finished to perform a consistency pass.

Drift compounds.

A slightly wrong page becomes the reference for the next slightly wrong page. Five generations later, the new version may be internally consistent — with the wrong character.

## Keep model pages

Choose two or three approved illustrations that best represent the book.

They should show useful differences:
- one clear face;
- one full-body view;
- one different angle or seated pose;
- companion together with the character when relevant.

These become your **model pages**.

Before approving a new generation, compare it against:

1. the canonical reference sheet;
2. the model pages;
3. the immediately neighboring pages.

The original prompt alone is not enough.

## Compare in the same order every time

A fast review sequence:

**Silhouette → age/proportion → hair → face → clothing → shoes/details → companion → rendering style**

A repeatable order catches problems faster than staring at two images and asking whether they "feel the same."

## Diagnose the drift

Say what changed.

Bad diagnosis:

> He looks different.

Useful diagnosis:

> His head is smaller relative to his body, his hair is longer at the neck, and the hoodie has become a zip-up jacket.

Now you know what to repair.

## Repair the variable, not the whole page

If the scene, composition and medium work, do not discard them because the shoes changed.

Correct the shoes.

If the dog markings drifted, repair the dog.

If the hair silhouette changed, repair the hair.

Regenerating the entire image gives the model another chance to break everything that was already correct.

## Avoid unnecessary anatomy risk

This production lesson applies directly to consistency work.

If the story does not require walking, running or a complex action pose, do not add one merely to make the page more dynamic.

Clean static silhouettes:
- are easier to compare;
- reduce limb/foot failures;
- preserve proportions;
- make character drift easier to see.

Movement should serve the story, not prove that the generator can draw movement.

# 14 — Character Reference Checklist

Use this before approving a recurring character on a finished page.

## IDENTITY
[ ] Apparent age matches  
[ ] Head/body proportions match  
[ ] Overall silhouette still reads as the same character  
[ ] Relative height matches recurring characters

## HAIR + FACE
[ ] Hair silhouette and length match  
[ ] Face shape and feature spacing match  
[ ] Facial rendering is no more detailed than the reference  
[ ] Expression stays within the book's established emotional range

## CLOTHING + ANCHORS
[ ] Core outfit silhouette matches  
[ ] Main colors match  
[ ] Shoes match  
[ ] Glasses, clips, hearing aids, jewelry or other identity anchors are correct  
[ ] No new logos, patterns, trim or decorative layers appeared

## COMPANION
[ ] Relative size matches  
[ ] Muzzle/ear/tail shapes match  
[ ] Coat length/texture matches  
[ ] Major markings stay in the correct places  
[ ] Collar/accessory matches

## STYLE
[ ] Pencil/paint behavior matches model pages  
[ ] Character has not become more polished or rendered  
[ ] Hair and clothing detail stay under the project's detail ceiling  
[ ] The character belongs beside the previous and next page

## FINAL QUESTION

> **If I removed the surrounding scene, would I still recognize this as the same character immediately?**

If not, diagnose the drift before moving on.
