# Writing a 60,000-Word Book with AI: Product Design

## Product definition

Create a standalone, tool-agnostic, 50-70-page PDF guide that teaches authors how to plan, draft, control, revise, and assemble a long-form manuscript of approximately 60,000 words with AI assistance.

The guide serves both fiction and nonfiction authors. It teaches one shared long-form workflow, then marks the points where fiction and nonfiction require different tools, evidence standards, and editorial checks.

The recurring nonfiction example is a book defining biblical manhood for sons and young men ages 13-21. The example makes the method concrete without turning the guide itself into a theology book. A smaller fiction example demonstrates character, plot, timeline, scene, and continuity techniques.

## Positioning

### Working title

**Writing a 60,000-Word Book with AI**

### Subtitle

**A practical system for planning, drafting, controlling, and revising a long-form manuscript without losing the plot - or your voice.**

### Core promise

The reader will leave with a repeatable system and the working documents needed to move from an idea to a coherent long-form manuscript. AI may generate options and pages, but the author remains responsible for the book's argument, structure, evidence, voice, judgment, and final language.

### Audience

- First-time and developing authors using generative AI
- Authors whose projects are too long for a single AI conversation
- Fiction writers concerned with plot, character, timeline, and continuity
- Nonfiction writers concerned with claims, evidence, sources, argument, and reader transformation
- Faith-based authors who need extra care around Scripture, interpretation, doctrine, and advice for young readers

### Format and voice

- 50-70 designed pages, targeting roughly 60 pages
- Practical and direct, not academic or promotional
- Tool-agnostic language that works with ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and future models
- Concrete examples, diagrams, checklists, worksheets, and copy-and-adapt prompts
- Clear labels distinguishing universal workflow, fiction-specific guidance, nonfiction-specific guidance, and biblical-project examples

## Content architecture

### Front matter: The real problem with long-form AI writing

Explain why a 60,000-word book cannot be produced responsibly with one giant prompt. Introduce the failure modes: context loss, repeated ideas, flattened voice, contradiction, fabricated facts, weak middles, drift from the original promise, and premature sentence polishing.

Establish the central principle: **Do not ask AI to remember the whole book. Build a system that remembers it for you.**

### Part I: Build the foundation

1. Define the book's promise, reader, purpose, and boundaries.
2. Choose the desired reader transformation.
3. Build a master project bible.
4. Define voice, tone, point of view, vocabulary, and prohibited habits.
5. Separate decisions already made from questions still open.
6. Establish research and source standards before drafting.

Fiction branch:

- Premise, genre promise, point of view, setting rules, character wants, stakes, arcs, and ending target

Nonfiction branch:

- Thesis, reader problem, argument, evidence standard, source hierarchy, exclusions, and intended application

Biblical-manhood example:

- Define the theological position and the specific meaning of "biblical manhood"
- Identify the primary reader while acknowledging the developmental difference between ages 13 and 21
- Create boundaries around doctrine, pastoral guidance, mental health, sexuality, relationships, and other sensitive advice
- Identify the Bible translation policy and rules for direct quotation, paraphrase, interpretation, and application

### Part II: Architect 60,000 words

1. Convert the core promise into parts, acts, or thematic sections.
2. Build a 20-30-chapter architecture.
3. Assign chapter functions and word budgets.
4. Create a chapter dependency map.
5. Write chapter briefs before prose.
6. Stress-test the outline for gaps, repetition, sequencing problems, and a weak middle.
7. Revise the architecture before drafting expensive prose.

The guide will include an example 60,000-word budget rather than treating 60,000 as a rigid requirement. It will show how chapter count and average chapter length can vary while preserving the total target.

Biblical-manhood example:

- Demonstrate a progression from identity and foundations to responsibility, relationships, discipline, courage, work, sexuality, failure, service, leadership, and mature faith
- Show how to avoid repeating the same principle under different chapter titles
- Use a repeatable chapter framework: Scripture in context, principle, contemporary situation, practical action, and reflection

### Part III: Build the manuscript operating system

Introduce the small set of documents that replace unreliable conversational memory:

- Master project bible
- Approved outline
- Chapter brief
- Style and voice sheet
- Decision log
- Continuity tracker
- Open-questions list
- Revision ledger
- Fiction character and timeline trackers
- Nonfiction claim-and-source ledger
- Biblical citation and interpretation ledger

Explain when each document is updated, what belongs in it, and what must remain stable. Provide compact context-packet templates for starting a new conversation without pasting the entire manuscript.

### Part IV: Draft chapter by chapter

1. Begin with a chapter brief, not a blank prompt.
2. Divide each chapter into beats, scenes, or argument moves.
3. Generate options before committing to prose.
4. Draft in manageable sections.
5. Review every section against the brief and project bible.
6. Update the decision log and trackers after each approved section.
7. End each chapter with a chapter summary and handoff packet.
8. Know when to continue a conversation and when to start a clean one.

Fiction branch:

- Scene purpose, conflict, change, character knowledge, emotional continuity, setup, payoff, and timeline

Nonfiction branch:

- Claim, evidence, source, explanation, example, objection, application, and transition

Biblical-manhood example:

- Never permit the model to invent or approximate verse wording
- Verify every quotation against the chosen translation
- Keep observation, interpretation, theological conclusion, and practical application visibly distinct during drafting
- Adjust vocabulary and scenarios for younger and older readers without speaking down to either group
- Use grounded situations involving school, work, family, friendships, dating, online behavior, temptation, failure, responsibility, and faith practice

### Part V: Revise in controlled passes

Teach revision as separate passes rather than one vague request to "make it better."

1. Promise and structure pass
2. Chapter-purpose and sequence pass
3. Continuity or argument pass
4. Evidence, citation, and factual-verification pass
5. Voice and audience pass
6. Repetition and compression pass
7. AI-tell pass
8. Sentence-level polish
9. Read-aloud and final human review

The guide will explicitly prohibit relying on AI as the final authority for facts, quotations, Scripture, doctrine, legal claims, medical claims, or other sensitive guidance.

Biblical-manhood example:

- Add a theological consistency review
- Add a Scripture-in-context review
- Add a youth-audience suitability review
- Recommend review by qualified human readers familiar with theology, youth development, and the intended faith tradition
- Flag shame-based language, simplistic promises, unsupported universals, and advice that could place a young reader at risk

### Part VI: Assemble and finish the book

1. Establish file names, version rules, and a chapter-status dashboard.
2. Assemble approved chapters into one manuscript.
3. Recheck transitions after assembly.
4. Create front matter, back matter, notes, bibliography, and acknowledgments as appropriate.
5. Prepare a clean beta-reader edition.
6. Collect feedback without surrendering the book's purpose.
7. Complete final source, permission, disclosure, and export checks.

6. Chapter-brief template

15. Chapter handoff summary

18. Final manuscript audit

## Prompt library

Prompts will be presented as adaptable frameworks rather than magic commands. Each prompt will state what documents to supply, what decision the author is making, what output format to request, and what the model must not decide.

The library will cover:

- Premise and thesis testing
- Outline stress-testing
- Chapter-brief creation
- Fiction scene options
- Nonfiction argument mapping
- Drafting from an approved brief
- Context reset and project handoff
- Continuity audit
- Source and claim audit
- Voice comparison
- Repetition detection
- Developmental edit
- Line edit with author-controlled constraints
- Final chapter summary and tracker update

## Worked examples

### Primary nonfiction example

A 60,000-word book defining biblical manhood for sons and young men ages 13-21. The example will recur throughout planning, architecture, chapter briefs, sourcing, drafting, audience calibration, and revision.

The example will demonstrate process, not prescribe a single denomination's conclusions. Any theological position used in sample material must be explicitly identified as an editorial choice to be made and reviewed by the book's author.

### Secondary fiction example

A compact fictional project used to demonstrate premise, character arcs, scene briefs, timeline control, setup and payoff, and continuity. It should be simple enough that readers can understand the example without learning a large fictional world.

## Visual design direction

The guide should feel related to the existing writing companion while being clearly more substantial and advanced:

- Warm paper background
- Deep green or navy structural color
- Restrained rust or gold accent
- Strong serif headings with clean sans-serif utility text
- Modular diagrams showing the manuscript pipeline
- Distinct badges for Fiction, Nonfiction, Biblical Project, Template, and Warning
- Generous margins and readable body type
- Worksheets designed for both printing and digital copying

## Quality and safety requirements

- No fabricated quotations, verses, citations, sources, or statistics
- No implication that AI can verify its own factual accuracy
- All examples clearly labeled as examples
- Bible quotations checked against the named translation before publication
- Clear separation between source text, interpretation, doctrine, and application
- No universal claim that one workflow or theological framing fits every author or faith tradition
- Sensitive advice for readers ages 13-21 receives explicit human-review guidance
- The guide itself remains useful to secular fiction and nonfiction authors
- No dependence on features unique to one AI vendor
- Every template must have an explained purpose and a worked example

## Definition of done

The finished product is ready when:

- The designed PDF is between 50 and 70 pages
- All six content parts are complete
- Both fiction and nonfiction paths are easy to follow
- The biblical-manhood example appears consistently across the nonfiction workflow
- Every promised worksheet and tracker is present and usable
- The prompt library is tool-agnostic
- The complete PDF has passed text, layout, link, page-number, consistency, and source-accuracy review
- A reader can use the guide to create a project bible, 60,000-word architecture, chapter briefs, context packets, trackers, revision plan, and final-manuscript checklist

## Out of scope for the first edition

- Writing or ghostwriting the reader's entire 60,000-word manuscript
- A denomination-specific theology of biblical manhood
- Publishing-platform setup, advertising, cover design, or book-launch strategy
- Legal conclusions about copyright or mandatory AI disclosure
- Tool-specific screenshots that will become obsolete quickly
- A database, app, or automated manuscript-management platform
