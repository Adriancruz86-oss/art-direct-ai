# The Environmental Cost of AI: Real Problems, Real Progress, and No Easy Answers

**Category:** Technology & Environment  
**Status:** Published  
**Product:** None — independent educational article  
**Updated:** September 22, 2026  
**Meta description:** AI has a real environmental footprint. Here's what the latest energy data says, what water and hardware add to the picture, and what solar panels, data centers, and electric vehicles teach us about technological progress.

Every time someone points out that AI uses electricity, somebody else responds that cars, streaming, video games, or the internet use electricity too.

Both statements can be true. Neither tells us whether AI's environmental impact is acceptable—or how to reduce it.

AI needs enormous computing infrastructure. Building it takes energy and materials. Running it requires electricity and cooling. And demand is rising faster than many power grids can expand.

At the same time, the amount of energy needed for an individual AI task has been falling quickly. AI can help scientists and engineers improve energy systems, too.

This is not a story in which AI is either an environmental disaster or a miraculous climate solution.

It is a story about **two curves racing each other: efficiency and demand**.

## Start with the numbers—and label them correctly

In its April 2026 update, the International Energy Agency (IEA) estimated that *all data centers worldwide* used about **485 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity in 2025**. Its central projection is roughly **950 TWh by 2030**, or about 3% of global electricity demand.

Those figures are for all data-center activity, not AI alone. Email, cloud storage, business software, video delivery, and traditional computing share this infrastructure.

But AI is becoming a significant driver of its growth. The same IEA report says electricity consumption at AI-focused data centers grew about **50% in 2025**, compared with **17% for data centers overall**.

There is a useful distinction here. The world's share consumed by data centers is still relatively modest. The rate at which that demand is growing is not.

A relatively small global percentage can also create a large local problem when several facilities are built near the same power lines, water supplies, and communities.

[Source: IEA, *Key Questions on Energy and AI*, April 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary).

## Electricity is not the same thing as carbon emissions

A data center using one megawatt-hour of electricity does not have the same carbon footprint everywhere.

It matters whether that electricity comes from coal, gas, wind, solar, hydro, or nuclear power. It also matters *when* the electricity is used. A company buying enough renewable-energy credits to cover its annual consumption is not the same as operating on carbon-free power every hour.

The IEA estimated in 2025 that electricity consumed by data centers produced around **180 million tonnes of indirect CO₂ emissions**, roughly **0.5% of global fuel-combustion emissions** at that time. It projected growth as data-center capacity expands. These are estimates for all data centers—not a measurement of AI's footprint alone.

Electricity demand can rise while emissions fall if the power supplying it gets cleaner fast enough. The reverse can happen if new facilities are supplied by additional fossil-fuel generation.

That is why a credible environmental claim needs both numbers: energy consumed and emissions associated with the actual power supply.

[Source: IEA, *Energy and AI: AI and Climate Change*, 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/ai-and-climate-change).

## And then there is water

Servers produce heat. Some data centers remove it with cooling systems that consume water through evaporation; others rely more heavily on air cooling or closed-loop designs.

Water is also used indirectly in electricity generation and in the manufacturing of chips and other equipment.

A viral claim about “one prompt using a bottle of water” is therefore a poor universal measure. The amount depends on the model, hardware, type of task, facility, cooling design, weather, and power source. Transparent, comparable measurements remain limited.

The local context matters enormously. A facility consuming water in a water-stressed region creates different risks from one using reclaimed water in a place with abundant supply.

Cooling design is a trade-off, not a magic switch: reducing on-site water consumption can sometimes increase electricity use, depending on the system and climate.

The relevant questions are practical. How much freshwater does the facility consume? How much is withdrawn and returned? What kind of water does it use? What happens during a drought? Who else depends on that supply?

[Source: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, *2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report*](https://bies.lbl.gov/publications/2024-lbnl-data-center-energy-usage-report).

## The footprint starts before the first prompt

An AI system does not appear out of thin air when you open a chatbot.

Its hardware requires mining, semiconductor fabrication, chemicals, manufacturing, shipping, and eventually disposal or recycling. Training a model involves substantial computation. Serving it to millions of people adds continuing operational demand.

Rapid hardware replacement can make the manufacturing footprint worse, even when each new chip is more efficient.

This is not an AI-only problem. The UN's *Global E-waste Monitor 2024* reported that the world generated **62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022**, while only **22.3%** was documented as formally collected and recycled. That is a global electronics figure, not an AI-hardware estimate, but it shows the larger waste system into which AI equipment enters.

The environmental discussion should include the entire lifecycle, not just a number for electricity per prompt.

[Source: ITU and UNITAR, *Global E-waste Monitor 2024*](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Environment/Pages/Publications/The-Global-E-waste-Monitor-2024.aspx).

## The other side: AI is getting more efficient very quickly

Here is the part that gets lost in some of the criticism.

The IEA's April 2026 analysis reports that advances in software and hardware have reduced energy use per AI task by **at least an order of magnitude annually in recent years**, depending on the task and comparison. Simple text queries are relatively lightweight compared with demanding uses such as AI video generation, extended reasoning, or agents running many operations.

That is significant progress.

But it does not automatically mean the industry's footprint is shrinking.

If the energy required for a task drops dramatically while the number of tasks increases even faster—and people start using far more demanding tools—total electricity consumption can still rise. Economists call this kind of effect a *rebound effect*.

The IEA says some emerging AI tasks can consume hundreds or thousands of times more energy per query than simple text generation. So a single universal claim about “the footprint of an AI query” is increasingly meaningless.

**Efficiency per task and total environmental impact are different measures.** We need to track both.

[Source: IEA, *Key Questions on Energy and AI*, April 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary).

## Haven't we seen this with other technologies?

Yes—with a critical qualification.

History offers examples of technologies whose environmental intensity fell as design, manufacturing, and power systems improved. It does **not** establish that every growing technology eventually reduces its total emissions.

Three comparisons are especially useful.

### 1. Solar panels: cleaner to manufacture, even as production grew

Solar panels require energy-intensive manufacturing, including the processing of silicon at high temperatures. Early growth raised legitimate questions about the emissions created before a panel generated its first unit of electricity.

The IEA found that innovation and changes in production **halved the emissions intensity of solar photovoltaic manufacturing between 2011 and 2021**. Improvements in energy and material efficiency played a major role.

But there is a catch: as production expanded dramatically, **total manufacturing emissions almost quadrupled** over the same period.

That is exactly the distinction we need to keep in mind with AI. A cleaner unit of technology does not guarantee a smaller industry-wide footprint when demand expands.

Solar panels also generate low-carbon electricity for decades after manufacture, so their lifecycle purpose and benefits differ substantially from AI computing. They are a useful example of manufacturing improvement, not proof that AI's environmental payoff will be similar.

[Source: IEA, *Solar PV Global Supply Chains*, 2022](https://www.iea.org/reports/solar-pv-global-supply-chains/executive-summary).

### 2. Traditional data centers: far more work for little additional energy—until demand accelerated

For a period, computing efficiency largely offset booming internet demand.

A 2020 study in *Science* estimated that global data-center compute instances rose **550% between 2010 and 2018**, while their electricity use rose by only about **6%**. More efficient servers, greater use of large cloud facilities, and better infrastructure helped decouple activity from energy demand.

That was a remarkable achievement.

And yet it would be misleading to extend that old trend indefinitely. AI accelerator demand has changed the picture. The IEA's more recent numbers show total data-center electricity consumption is now rising quickly again.

The lesson is not “technology always solves its footprint.” It is that efficiency improvements are real, measurable, and capable of being overwhelmed by new demand.

[Source: Masanet and colleagues, *Recalibrating global data center energy-use estimates*, Science, 2020](https://datacenters.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/Masanet_et_al_Science_2020.full_.pdf).

### 3. Electric vehicles and batteries: manufacturing choices and cleaner power matter

Electric vehicles have an emissions cost before anyone drives them. Battery materials have to be mined, processed, and assembled. The footprint varies with chemistry, manufacturing methods, and electricity sources.

The IEA's 2024 analysis found that lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery packs had roughly **one-third lower emissions per kilowatt-hour at the pack level** than the high-nickel battery chemistry it compared them with. Cleaner electricity, higher energy density, and recycling can reduce the footprint further.

The IEA projected that under its Announced Pledges Scenario, battery lifecycle emissions could fall about **35% by 2035** for the chemistries analyzed. That figure is a scenario, not an accomplished reduction.

This example shows how design and supply-chain choices can lower carbon intensity. It also shows why progress needs to be measured, rather than assumed.

[Source: IEA, *Global EV Outlook 2024: Outlook for Emissions Reductions*](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2024/outlook-for-emissions-reductions).

## Could AI help the environment enough to offset its own costs?

Potentially. But anyone promising that outcome today is jumping ahead of the evidence.

AI can help forecast electricity demand, optimize electricity grids, detect equipment faults, improve industrial processes, support scientific research, and improve weather forecasting.

The IEA's 2025 *Energy and AI* report estimated that widespread adoption of existing AI-enabled solutions **could** produce emissions reductions equivalent to around **5% of energy-related emissions in 2035**. That is a modeled opportunity, not a demonstrated net benefit. Adoption barriers, rebound effects, and the energy consumed by AI itself all matter.

And some AI applications may increase emissions—for example, by making resource-intensive activities cheaper or more convenient.

The honest position is to count benefits when they materialize, count costs as they occur, and avoid treating hypothetical future savings as permission to ignore present-day impacts.

[Source: IEA, *Energy and AI: Executive Summary*, 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary).

## What would responsible progress actually look like?

It is not enough for a company to announce that its next model needs less energy per response.

Meaningful progress would include clearer public reporting of total electricity use, relevant emissions, local water consumption, and the manufacturing footprint of equipment. Measurements should distinguish simple chat from more demanding workloads and separate AI-specific estimates from data-center totals.

Infrastructure matters too: cleaner electricity, efficient hardware and software, sensible facility locations, cooling suited to local water conditions, longer hardware life, and better reuse and recycling.

It also matters who bears the costs. A new data center can impose grid-upgrade or water-supply pressures on the area around it. The economic benefits and environmental burden should not be assumed to land on the same people.

Individual users can make reasonable choices as well: use the tool when it genuinely adds value, avoid generating endless disposable outputs without purpose, and prefer a simpler task when it accomplishes the same thing. But the largest levers are industrial—power procurement, facility design, hardware, transparency, and demand—not guilt over a single ordinary question.

## Progress is possible. It is not automatic.

Two extreme statements dominate this discussion:

“AI is destroying the planet.”

“AI will become efficient, so the environmental impact doesn't matter.”

Neither is a useful substitute for measurement.

AI has a real and growing footprint. Some of its applications may help lower emissions elsewhere. Other emerging technologies show that carbon intensity can improve dramatically. They also show that total emissions may rise when demand grows faster than efficiency.

So the important question is not whether AI will eventually become more efficient. It already has.

The important question is whether **efficiency, cleaner energy, responsible construction, and transparent reporting can keep pace with the scale at which we choose to use it**.

That outcome is not guaranteed by innovation alone.

It is something we have to build.
